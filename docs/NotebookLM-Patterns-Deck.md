### Slide 2: Money Is a Liquid

**Background Image Prompt:** A photo-realistic image of a beautiful, cute 16-year-old female Thai student (long hair tied back with a white bow, white shirt featuring only the Thai letters "ส.ว." and a shield badge above it strictly on the protagonist's right side / viewer's left). She crouches beside a dark drain in a wet night street, watching glowing cyan liquid light pour away down it, some of it slipping through her cupped fingers. Reflective wet ground, cinematic teal lighting.

**Title:** Money Is a Liquid
**Subtitle:** The metaphor that decides your prepositions

**Foreground Diagram — "The Hydraulic Map":** A single vertical plumbing schematic in glowing cyan. At the top, a tap pouring liquid, labelled **OUT OF** with the caption "run OUT OF money". In the middle, a container filling, labelled **INTO** with the caption "pay INTO an account". At the bottom, a dark drain swallowing the flow, labelled **DOWN** with the caption "throw money DOWN the drain". To the right, a small splashing hand icon labelled **SPLASH OUT**. Thin connector lines link each phrase to its part of the plumbing.

**Content:**
- English does not compare money to water. It treats money **as** water, and that is why the prepositions are what they are.
- Money is **splashed** out, it goes **down** the drain, it **runs out**, it **flows** in and out. The liquid form of wealth is literally called **cash**, and bankers call it a **liquid** asset.
- Predict the preposition from the picture: liquid goes DOWN a drain, OUT OF a tap, INTO a container.
- Water down a drain cannot be collected again. That is why **throw money down the drain** means the loss is total and permanent.

**Synthesis Takeaway:** Take any water verb you know — pour, leak, dry up, trickle, flood — and say it with money. The ones that sound natural are the metaphor working for you.

**Content:**

### Slide 7: Cheap and Good Value Are Different Axes

**Background Image Prompt:** A photo-realistic image of a beautiful, cute 16-year-old female Thai student (long hair tied back with a white bow, white shirt featuring only the Thai letters "ส.ว." and a shield badge above it strictly on the protagonist's right side / viewer's left). She holds up an old brass balance scale. On one pan sits a cheap flimsy phone charger already sparking and breaking; on the other sits a solid, well-made leather school bag glowing softly. The cheap item is the heavier one, tipping the scale the wrong way. Dark shop interior.

**Title:** Cheap and Good Value Are Different Axes
**Subtitle:** Price is one measurement. Worth is another.

**Foreground Diagram — "The Value Matrix":** A clean 2×2 grid. Vertical axis labelled **WORTH** (low at bottom, high at top). Horizontal axis labelled **PRICE** (low at left, high at right). Fill the four quadrants exactly: top-left, cyan — **A BARGAIN / A STEAL** (low price, high worth), captioned "snap it up"; top-right, cyan-gold — **GOOD VALUE FOR MONEY** (high price, high worth), captioned "boots that last five years"; bottom-left, grey — **A WASTE OF MONEY** (low price, low worth), captioned "a fake charger that dies in a week"; bottom-right, red — **A RIP-OFF / COSTS AN ARM AND A LEG** (high price, low worth). Beneath the grid, a thin horizontal price ladder: dirt cheap → a steal → a bargain → in the sale → with a discount → full price → overpriced → a rip-off.

**Content:**
- **Cheap** only reports that the number is low. **Good value for money** reports that the worth is high compared with the number. Most learners collapse the two into one.
- An expensive thing can be excellent value, and a cheap thing can be a **rip-off**.
- A **rip-off** is not simply a high price. It is a high price for low worth, which is why a 1,000-baht bag can be a bargain and a 50-baht one can be a rip-off.
- A **discount** and a **sale** describe a **movement downwards** from the normal price, not a level. Twenty per cent off an overpriced thing is still overpriced.

**Synthesis Takeaway:** Name one thing you own that was expensive AND good value, and one that was cheap AND a waste of money. If you can do both in ten seconds, you own the distinction for life.

### Slide 8: Learn the Family, Not the Word

**Background Image Prompt:** A photo-realistic image of a beautiful, cute 16-year-old female Thai student (long hair tied back with a white bow, white shirt featuring only the Thai letters "ส.ว." and a shield badge above it strictly on the protagonist's right side / viewer's left). She looks up at a glowing holographic tree growing out of a single seed on her open palm, its branches ending in different word-forms of the same root. Dark library, warm gold and cyan light.

**Title:** Learn the Family, Not the Word
**Subtitle:** English reshapes old words far more often than it invents new ones

**Foreground Diagram — "The Morphology Tree":** A three-trunk tree. TRUNK 1, root **RIP OFF**, splits into two branches: verb — "they **ripped** me **off**" (two words) and noun — "a **rip-off**" (hyphen); a small rule tag reads "hyphen in the noun, two words in the verb". TRUNK 2, root **RE- = BACK**, splits into three branches: **re**fund · **re**pay · **re**imburse, with a cyan return-arrow beside each, and a tag reading "pay back is the everyday English version of all three". TRUNK 3, root **WASTE**, splits into verb — "he **wasted** 800 baht" and noun — "it was a **waste of** 800 baht"; the tag reads "the frame changes, the spelling does not".

**Content:**
- Phrasal verbs become nouns by joining up, usually with a hyphen: the tickets **sold out** → it was a **sell-out**.
- The Latin prefix **RE-** means *back*, and it gives one single direction to **refund**, **repay** and **reimburse**.
- **Purchase** never changes shape at all: make a **purchase** (noun), **purchase** the tickets (verb). **Pay** gives payment, repay, payback.
- **Value** gives value (noun), valuable (adjective) and value for money (adjective phrase) — but never, mark this one with a red cross, "a valuable price".

**Synthesis Takeaway:** For any new money word, answer three questions in writing: what is the noun, what is the verb, and does the noun take a hyphen?

### Slide 10: Substance Words and Object Words

**Background Image Prompt:** A photo-realistic image of a beautiful, cute 16-year-old female Thai student (long hair tied back with a white bow, white shirt featuring only the Thai letters "ส.ว." and a shield badge above it strictly on the protagonist's right side / viewer's left). In her left hand she holds a clear glass beaker of glowing cyan liquid; in her right hand, three solid gold coins held up separately between her fingertips. Dark science laboratory, precise clinical lighting.

**Title:** Substance Words and Object Words
**Subtitle:** Exactly like water and bottles

**Foreground Diagram — "Water and Bottles":** Two panels divided by a vertical line. LEFT panel, cyan, headed **THE SUBSTANCE — uncountable**, showing a beaker of liquid, listing: money, cash, change, with the permitted quantifiers underneath: some · much · a lot of · any, and a red-crossed row: "a cash" ✗ · "cashes" ✗ · "a money" ✗ · "much coins" ✗. Beneath both panels, a single wide caption strip: "**change** is the awkward one — it names both the coins in your pocket and the money handed back at the till, and **both senses are uncountable**."

**Content:**
- English forces a division here that nobody explains: the **uncountable** words name the substance, the **countable** ones name the objects made of it.
- Right: some **cash**, a lot of **money**, have you got any **change**, three **coins**, two 100-baht **notes**, four monthly **payments**, a recent **purchase**.
- Wrong, with red crosses: "a cash", "cashes", "a money", "changes" when you mean coins, "much coins".
- **Spending habits** runs the other way and is almost always plural. One habit is a choice; habits are a personality.

**Synthesis Takeaway:** Say five true sentences about what is in your bag right now, using *some*, *a lot of*, *any*, *three* and *two*. If the word accepts a number, it is countable.

### Slide 12: Pairs One Letter Apart

**Background Image Prompt:** A photo-realistic image of a beautiful, cute 16-year-old female Thai student (long hair tied back with a white bow, white shirt featuring only the Thai letters "ส.ว." and a shield badge above it strictly on the protagonist's right side / viewer's left). She holds a large magnifying glass up to two almost identical glowing shop signs hanging side by side, one reading ON SALE and one reading FOR SALE. Her eye is enlarged and sharply focused through the lens. Dark shopfront at night.

**Title:** Pairs One Letter Apart
**Subtitle:** Most errors here are confusions, not gaps

**Foreground Diagram — "The Trap Table":** A two-column comparison table with six rows. Print the distinguishing element of each pair in red so the eye lands on it. Row 1: **ON** sale = the price has been reduced / **FOR** sale = you may buy it. Row 2: **ECONOMIC** = about the economy / **ECONOMICAL** = saves you money. Row 3: **CUT DOWN** = do less of it / **CUT OUT** = stop completely. Row 4: **BORROW** = taking (I borrowed 100 baht **FROM** her) / **LEND** = giving (she lent 100 baht **TO** me). Row 5: **CHEAP** = a low price / **GOOD VALUE** = high worth for the price. Row 6: **SWIPE** = pay by card at the till / **SWIPE** = steal ("someone swiped my pen"). Beneath the table, a separate strip headed **SILENT LETTERS — write it, never say it**: recei**p**t /ri-SEET/ and de**b**t /det/, with the silent letter greyed out and struck through in red. Beside that, a four-way fan: a **FEE** is for a service · a **PRICE** is for a thing · a **FINE** is a punishment · a **TAX** goes to the government.

**Content:**
- The shoes are **for sale** (you may buy them) and they are **on sale** (the price has been reduced). Only one of those is good news.
- Four different reasons for handing over the same coins: a **fee**, a **price**, a **fine**, a **tax**.
- Only the **borrower** pays back: I borrowed 100 baht **from** her, she **lent** 100 baht **to** me, and then I **paid her back**.
- Write the silent letter, never sound it: **receipt** and **debt**.

**Synthesis Takeaway:** Put the six trap pairs on one card, questions on the front and answers on the back, and test yourself once a day for a week. Sixty seconds a day fixes more marks than an hour of new words.

**Foreground Diagram — "The Splitting Prism":** Two prisms stacked vertically, plus one reversed. PRISM 1: the Thai word **เก็บ (kep)** enters as one white beam and exits as three separate beams labelled **SAVE** money for later · **SET ASIDE** money for one purpose · **KEEP** the change as a tip. PRISM 2: the Thai pair **ยืม (yuem)** and **ให้ยืม (hai yuem, literally give-borrow)** enters and exits as two English beams labelled **BORROW** (I borrow **from** you) and **LEND** (you lend **to** me), with a red-crossed caption beneath: "Can you borrow me 100 baht?" ✗. PRISM 3, drawn reversed to show English is sometimes the vaguer language: two Thai beams enter and merge into the single English word **CHANGE**, captioned "the coins in your pocket AND the money handed back at the till".

**Content:**
- Thai **เก็บ (kep)** covers both *keep* and *save*, so Thai speakers reach for **keep** where English needs **save** or **set aside**.
- Thai builds *lend* out of *borrow* — **ยืม** becoming **ให้ยืม** — which is exactly why "Can you borrow me 100 baht?" is such a common slip.
- Thai counts with classifiers and has no articles, so nothing in your first language warns you that "a cash" is impossible. English makes you choose: some cash, three coins, money in an account.
- Going the other way, **English** is the vaguer language here: **change** covers two things that Thai keeps separate.

**Synthesis Takeaway:** Translate three Thai money sentences you would genuinely say into English, then check two things only: did you choose save, set aside or keep correctly, and is every uncountable word free of *a* and free of *s*?

**Subtitle:** Not a list to memorise. A chain, in order.

**Foreground Diagram — "The Cash Chain":** One horizontal chain of seven numbered nodes connected by cyan arrows, left to right: **1 ACCOUNT** (money rests here) → **2 CARD** (gives access) → **3 ATM or TILL** (the release point) → **4 SWIPE / TAKE OUT** (the action) → **5 NOTES & COINS** (the physical money) → **6 CHANGE** (what comes back) → **7 RECEIPT** (proof the step happened). **Synthesis Takeaway:** Narrate your last real trip to a cash machine in six sentences, one per step, using a different word from the chain in each sentence. Any step you cannot name is your gap.
